[930] Chroma Key — Green Screen and Blue Screen <span class="lv">●●○</span>
[931] You film an actor in front of green or blue cloth, remove only that color, and combine them with another background. AE’s Keylight does the removing, but 80% of the result is decided on set.
[932] 8-1 Why Green?
[933] Chroma key picks “a color people hardly have” and removes only that color. Human skin of any ethnicity mixes red and yellow tones, so green and blue, on the opposite side, separate best. Green has become today’s standard for three reasons.
[934] Reason
[935] Digital sensors are strong in green
[936] On most camera sensors (Bayer pattern), 2 of every 4 pixels capture green. Green has the most information and the least noise, so edges key cleanly.
[937] Bright, so less light is needed
[938] Under the same light, green records brighter than blue. A large screen is easier to light evenly.
[939] Rarely clashes with clothing
[940] Blue clothes such as jeans and suits are common, but fluorescent green clothes are rare.
[941] 8-2 When Blue Screen Is Still Used
[942] Situation
[943] The film era
[944] On film, the blue-sensitive layer had finer grain, so most films from the 1970s to the 1990s used blue screen. The flying scenes in the 1978 film <i>Superman</i> are a classic example.
[945] Night scenes and dark backgrounds
[946] Green is bright, so spill — green light bleeding onto the actor’s edges — is strong. Blue spill is less noticeable and blends well with dark backgrounds.
[947] Blonde or light hair, green props
[948] When green reflections show in the hair, or green clothes or plants get removed too.
[949] Water and sea scenes
[950] As in <i>Life of Pi</i>, when skies and horizons in large water-tank shoots are handled with a blue background.
[951] <b>Only one rule</b>Choose a color that the actor and props don’t have. Blue for an actor in green; green for a blue uniform. Occasionally red or magenta backgrounds are used.
[952] 8-3 Shooting for a Clean Key — Lighting Setup
[953] <b>Figure 8-1</b>Green-screen lighting layout (seen from above; generated with Higgsfield GPT Image and labeled). Screen lights ①② light only the screen evenly, not the actor; actor lights ③④⑤ are placed so they don’t hit the screen. The actor stands 2–3 m from the screen to avoid green reflections (spill) and shadows.
[954] <b>Figure 8-2</b>This is how it looks in a real studio (an example made with Higgsfield GPT Image). There are separate lights for the screen and for the person, and the person stands away from the screen.
[955] Most keying trouble comes from an “uneven screen” and “green light on the actor”. Set up in this order.
[956] <b>Light the screen and the actor separately.</b> Light the screen evenly, as if painting it, from above and below on both sides with broad soft lights (softboxes, fluorescent banks). Light the actor with separate key and fill lights.
[957] <b>Keep the actor 2–3 m from the screen.</b> If too close, green reflected light lands on the shoulders and head (spill) and shadows fall on the screen.
[958] <b>Match the brightness.</b> Keep the brightness difference across the whole screen within 1/3 stop. On the camera’s waveform or false color it should show as one flat line. Slightly darker than, or similar to, the actor’s face is a safe level.
[959] <b>Add backlight (rim light).</b> Light from above and behind the actor brings out the hair outline and covers green spill. A slightly warm color (amber, light magenta) cancels spill.
[960] <b>Match the light direction to the background.</b> If the sun is on the left in the background footage, put the actor’s key light on the left too. Even a perfect key looks like a “pasted-in person” if the light direction differs.
[961] <b>Camera settings</b>: use a fast shutter (1/100 or faster) to reduce motion blur, and record in 4:2:2 or better at a high bitrate if possible. Heavily compressed files break edges into stair steps.
